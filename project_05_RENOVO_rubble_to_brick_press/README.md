**James Dyson Award 2026**

## The Problem

After earthquakes, rubble is abandoned or expensively removed while communities import new bricks at massive carbon and financial cost. The waste and the need for materials sit side by side, unconnected.

## The Solution

A compact, solar-electric or human-powered press that crushes concrete rubble, mixes it with geopolymer binder (no kiln), and produces standard compressed rubble bricks on-site within 48 hours.

### ❓ How it works
*(Limit: 975 characters)*
he press operates in three stages. First, a jaw-crusher attachment (electric or manual flywheel driven) reduces concrete rubble to 5–20 mm aggregate through an integral sieve that removes rebar and debris. Second, the aggregate enters a mixing chamber with a geopolymer binder solution — sodium silicate activates fly ash or ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS) into a cementitious matrix with no firing required. The mix ratio is 75% crushed rubble, 25% geopolymer paste by volume.

Third, the mixture is loaded into the compression mold and pressed at 10–15 MPa via hydraulic ram — powered by a small solar panel and battery bank, or by manual lever for off-grid operation. The brick is ejected and stacked for 24–48 hour ambient curing, achieving 15–25 MPa compressive strength — meeting standard requirements for single-story residential construction.

### ❓ How you developed your design | Design Process
*(Limit: 1300 characters)*

The design began with three field constraints: transportable by small truck, operable by unskilled labor, and manufacturable from standard steel sections available in any country.

Material research focused on geopolymer chemistry as the binder. Portland cement was eliminated — it requires controlled curing conditions and does not bind recycled aggregate reliably. Alkali-activated geopolymers cure at ambient temperature and achieve equivalent strength within 24 hours. Fly ash and GGBS — industrial byproducts available globally — were selected as precursors for their low cost and wide availability in post-earthquake regions.

The mechanical architecture is modular: jaw crusher, mixing drum, and compression press are separate units that bolt together in the field, allowing damaged components to be replaced individually. Drive options were designed in parallel — 1.5 kW electric motor (200 W solar panel sufficient) and a manual flywheel — ensuring operation without grid power.

Brick dimensions were standardized to ISO 228 × 108 × 76 mm to ensure drop-in compatibility with conventional masonry, so reconstruction teams need no retraining. Strength targets were validated against published geopolymer literature: 15–25 MPa compressive strength is achievable with 75% recycled aggregate at 10–15 MPa press pressure.

### ❓ What sets it apart
*(Limit: 975 characters)*

Existing post-earthquake rubble management has two options: pay for expensive removal and landfill, or leave rubble in place for years. Some NGOs use manual rubble-sorting for road fill, but no field-deployable system exists to upcycle concrete rubble into structural bricks.

Geopolymer compressed earth block presses exist for new-build construction but use virgin soil, not recycled concrete rubble — and are not designed for disaster-site deployment or rubble pre-processing.

The Rubble-to-Brick Press is the first integrated system combining on-site rubble crushing, geopolymer mixing, and compression in a single deployable unit. It eliminates material transportation costs in both directions: rubble stays on-site and new bricks need not be imported. The economic case is compelling: post-earthquake brick prices spike 300–500% due to demand — rubble bricks cost only the energy and binder to produce them.

### ❓ What the future holds for it
*(Limit: 650 characters)*

The first prototype will be validated with concrete rubble from demolition sites, testing compressive strength across varying aggregate types and contamination levels. Field trials are planned with disaster relief NGOs in earthquake-prone regions. A lightweight flat-pack version will be developed for air-freight delivery in the first 72 hours after a disaster — before road access is restored. Long-term, the binder chemistry will be refined to work with broader rubble types including brick, tile, and mixed debris, expanding the addressable waste stream significantly.
