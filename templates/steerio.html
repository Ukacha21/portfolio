STEERIO — DIY USB MECHATRONICS STEERING RIG 600 PPR Continuous Optical Encoder, 74HC165 Multiplexed Wheel Hub, Full Truck Switch Cluster & Dual Analog Pedals

An end-to-end mechatronic driving simulator rig built for Euro Truck Simulator 2 and recognized by Windows as a single plug-and-play USB HID game controller named "steerIO". Engineered around an STM32F103 "Blue Pill" running open-source FreeJoy firmware, the system integrates a 600 PPR optical rotary encoder with a 12-wire slip ring for unlimited continuous rotation, an on-wheel 74HC165 shift register that collapses 8 push buttons down to 3 digital lines, sequential paddle shifters, a realistic truck switch cluster (blinkers, hazards, wipers, lighting controls, engine start/stop, and parking brake), an analog cabin look stick, and a detachable 2-pedal potentiometer box connected via a rugged DE-9 interface.

Controller: STM32F103C8T6 (ARM Cortex-M3 @ 72 MHz, USB-C) Steering Sensor: 600 PPR Optical Rotary Encoder (Hardware Fast Encoder) Rotation Freedom: 360° Continuous (12-Wire Flanged Slip Ring) Wheel Multiplexing: 74HC165 8-Bit PISO Shift Register (3 Digital Lines) Digital Inputs: 17 Inputs (8 Buttons, 2 Paddles, 6 Cluster Levers, 1 Joy Click) Analog Axes: 5 Axes (Steering, Throttle, Brake, Camera Look X/Y) Pedal Quick-Release: Detachable Male/Female DE-9 (DB9) D-Sub Mechanical Bearings: UCP 204 Pillow Block, 6000zz & 625zz Ball Bearings

🔄

12-Wire Slip Ring Junction

Enables continuous 360° rotation without mechanical stops or winding cables. Uses 8 conductors for 3.3V, GND, 3 shift register lines, and 3 joystick lines, leaving 4 lines spare for future dash displays.

⚡

74HC165 Hub Multiplexer

Collects 8 momentary buttons on the wheel and serializes them onto 3 digital lines (CLK, LATCH, DATA), reducing slip-ring conductor count by over 60%.

⏱️

Hardware Fast Encoder

Routes optical encoder A/B channels to STM32 hardware timer capture pins (PA8 / PA9). Edge counting is handled directly in silicon, preventing count drops during rapid steering wheel spins.

🔌

DE-9 Modular Pedal Interface

Detachable 9-pin industrial connector connects the standalone throttle and brake assembly to the main unit, allowing quick breakdown, transport, and servicing.

Engineering Note on Pedal Inputs (U3 & U4) Hall Effect → WX14 Potentiometer Transition

Schematic vs. Final Hardware: In the KiCad schematic above, the pedal inputs (U3 and U4) are depicted as analog Hall Effect sensors with the design note "MAKE SURE HALL EFFECT IS PROPORTIONAL AND NOT THRESHOLD BASED SWITCHES". While Hall Effect sensors offer frictionless contactless operation, their linear magnetic flux sensing zone required the physical pedal throw to be impractically short.

A short, shallow pedal stroke made realistic driving modulation difficult in simulator testing. Consequently, the Hall Effect sensors were ditched and replaced with industrial WX14 wire-wound potentiometers (10 kΩ throttle, 470 Ω brake). This prompted a complete ground-up mechanical redesign of the pedal mechanism (located in pedals_newDesign/ind_trimpot) to house the WX14 potentiometers with articulated linkages and 625zz bearings for a generous, realistic 150°–160° travel angle.

Practical Insights

Engineering Challenges & Debugging Logs

Real-world hardware troubleshooting, signal conditioning solutions, and simulator integration fixes.

01

STM32 Blue Pill Clone USB-C Cable Incompatibility

Symptom: The board failed to enumerate on modern USB-C to USB-C laptop ports; the 3.3V power rails measured 0V.

Root Cause: Many commercially sold "USB-C" Blue Pill modules are hardware clones that omit the two mandatory 5.1 kΩ pull-down resistors on the Type-C Configuration Channel (CC1 / CC2) pins. Modern USB-C host power delivery controllers cannot detect device attachment without these resistors.

Solution: Always connect the microcontroller to the host using a standard USB Type-A to USB Type-C cable, where power delivery is statically asserted.

02

Strict 3.3V Logic Rail vs 5V Tolerance on STM32F103

Safety Rule: On the STM32F103, GPIO pins shared with the internal ADC (PA0–PA7, PB0, PB1) are strictly 3.3V max and NOT 5V-tolerant. Connecting a 5V signal destroys the ADC multiplexer.

Implementation: The pedal potentiometers, joystick module, and 74HC165 shift register are fed exclusively from the regulated 3.3V rail. The 600 PPR optical encoder runs on 5V, but its phase A/B outputs are routed specifically to PA8 and PA9, which are verified 5V-tolerant GPIOs.

03

Encoder Pulse Dropping During Rapid Counter-Steering

Symptom: Software polling of optical encoder edges missed transitions during quick steering corrections, causing angle drift and reverse direction reads.

Solution: Configured PA8 and PA9 as FreeJoy's hardware Fast Encoder input. Transitions are decoded directly by STM32 hardware timer counters in silicon, guaranteeing zero missed counts even during high-velocity drifts.

04

Wire-Wound Potentiometer End-Track Contact Noise

Challenge: Industrial wire-wound potentiometers exhibit wiper chatter and discrete step transitions when pressed fully to physical travel limits (0% and 100%), creating calibration drift.

Solution: Implemented mechanical travel stops that avoid the extreme ends of the track, paired with software 3% to 5% deadband margins at both minimum and maximum travel endpoints in FreeJoy.

05

Sequential Paddle Shifter Mapping in Euro Truck Simulator 2

Issue: Binding paddles to numbered "Shifter Position 1/2" caused the virtual transmission to snap back into neutral immediately upon releasing the paddle.

Fix: In ETS2 Options > Controls, set transmission mode to Sequential and bind the paddle microswitches to Shift Up and Shift Down commands.

06

Shift Register Polarity & Logic Inversion

Challenge: The 8 on-wheel push buttons use external 10 kΩ pull-downs to GND (Active HIGH), while the stationary microswitches use the STM32's internal pull-up resistors (Active LOW).

Solution: Resolved completely in software using FreeJoy's per-button Invert mask, ensuring unified logical button events without requiring hardware NOT gates.

07

Pedal Kinematics: Ditching Hall Effect for WX14 Wire-Wound Potentiometers

Travel Arc vs. Sensor Range: The original KiCad schematic utilized Hall Effect sensors (U3 & U4) for throttle and brake sensing. Although Hall Effect sensors provide frictionless contactless operation, maintaining proportional linearity requires the magnet to stay in a narrow millimeter-scale air gap. This constrained pedal travel to an impractically short, shallow throw that felt unrealistic in-game.

Mechanical Redesign: The Hall Effect approach was ditched in favor of industrial WX14 wire-wound potentiometers. To house the WX14 units, the entire pedal mechanism was completely redesigned from scratch (pedals_newDesign/ind_trimpot) with dual 625zz ball bearings, reinforced pivot arms, and calibrated linkage geometry—delivering an expansive 150° to 160° travel angle with progressive tactile resistance.
